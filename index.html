Climate Patterns in Action

Climate includes average conditions, seasonal variability, and long-term patterns of extreme weather. Explore how those patterns differ across places, seasons, and decades.

Three ways to view climate patterns

An individual heat wave, flood, or storm is weather. When scientists examine the kinds of extreme weather that occur, where and when they occur, and how their frequency or intensity changes over many years, those events become part of the climate pattern.

Comparison 3

Many years. Changing patterns.

The first comparisons focused on individual events. EPA climate indicators use long-term observations to examine changes in patterns of extreme weather.

EPA reports observed U.S. changes that include more extreme heat, less extreme cold, more heavy precipitation, and increased potential for intense hurricanes. Trends in drought, wildfire, flooding, and other extremes can differ by region, time period, and measurement.

Climate change does not make every type of extreme event increase everywhere in the same way.

Heavy precipitation

Becoming more common nationally

EPA tracks unusually large amounts of rain or snow falling in a short period. What counts as heavy depends on location and season.

Extreme heat and cold

Heat increasing; extreme cold decreasing

Both changes are part of a warming climate pattern.

Drought and wildfire

Varies by region and indicator

Patterns depend on precipitation, temperature, soil moisture, vegetation, and location.

Tropical cyclones

Intensity and rainfall are key measures

Scientists examine rainfall, intensity, and rapid intensification—not only storm counts.

Source: EPA, Climate Change Indicators in the United States, Fifth Edition. Open the EPA report.

Key takeaway

An individual extreme event is weather. Patterns in the kinds, timing, location, frequency, and intensity of extreme weather over many years are part of climate.
