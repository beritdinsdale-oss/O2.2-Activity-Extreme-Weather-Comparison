Climate includes extreme weather

Climate is not limited to average temperature and precipitation. It also includes patterns in the types, timing, frequency, and intensity of extreme weather.

How could the same summer bring extreme heat to one part of the country and record flooding to another?

☀

Pacific Northwest · Summer 2021

Extreme heat

116°F Portland's record high during the June 2021 heat event

What happened?

  • Record temperatures affected western Oregon and the broader Pacific Northwest.
  • Communities opened cooling centers and faced severe health risks.
  • Infrastructure, trees, landscapes, and gardens experienced heat stress.

Climate connection

A warmer climate raises the temperature baseline. When a weather pattern produces hot conditions, it can now produce more intense and record-breaking heat.

Evidence meter

Strong evidence

Scientists have high confidence that human-caused warming increases the likelihood and intensity of extreme heat.

Waverly, Tennessee · Summer 2021

Record rainfall and flash flooding

20.73 in. Tennessee's verified 24-hour rainfall record near the event area

What happened?

  • More than 20 inches of rain fell within 24 hours near Waverly.
  • Flash flooding devastated homes, roads, and communities.
  • This summer hazard was very different from Pacific Northwest heat.

Climate connection

Warmer air can hold more water vapor. When storms form, that added moisture can contribute to heavier downpours and greater flood risk.

Evidence meter

Strong evidence

Heavy precipitation has increased in many parts of the United States, although the amount of change differs by region.

Willamette Valley · Winter 2021

Ice storm

Up to 1.5 in. Reported ice accumulation in portions of the region

What happened?

  • Freezing rain coated roads, trees, and power lines with ice.
  • Falling limbs and damaged equipment caused widespread outages.
  • Some communities remained without power for days.

Climate connection

A warming climate does not eliminate winter weather. Cold conditions still occur, while the timing, location, and characteristics of winter hazards may change.

Evidence meter

Evidence still evolving

Researchers are still learning how climate change affects freezing rain and ice storms in particular regions.

Willamette Valley · Summer 2021

Extreme heat

116°F Portland's record high during the June 2021 heat event

What happened?

  • Temperatures reached levels far outside typical summer conditions.
  • The heat affected people, infrastructure, plants, and ecosystems.
  • The summer hazard contrasted sharply with the winter ice storm.

Climate connection

Higher average temperatures make unusually hot conditions more likely. The connection between warming and extreme heat is direct and well established.

Evidence meter

Strong evidence

Scientists are much more confident about the influence of climate change on extreme heat than on regional ice-storm frequency.

Why do the probabilities change?

1Greenhouse gases warm the climate.

2Weather develops within those warmer conditions.

3The likelihood or intensity of some extremes changes.

4Scientists detect those patterns over many years.

Climate change does not affect every hazard equally or produce the same result in every region.

Evidence meter: the larger pattern

Strong evidence

Long-term observations provide strong evidence that human-caused climate change is altering the frequency or intensity of several types of extreme weather. Scientists assess each hazard separately because the mechanisms and evidence differ.

The climate picture

1

Regional variation

Seasonal variation

One region can experience different hazards during different seasons.

3

Long-term change

Decades of observations reveal changes in frequency and intensity.

Average conditions + Regional and seasonal variation + Patterns of extreme weather = Climate

Key takeaway

An individual extreme event is weather. Patterns in the types, timing, location, frequency, and intensity of extreme weather over many years are part of climate. As climate changes, the conditions in which weather develops also change—but the evidence is stronger for some hazards than for others.
